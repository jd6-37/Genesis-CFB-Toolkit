In the original Bill Walsh College Football ’95, a halfback who carries the ball three times in a row drops to about 50% energy on Team Rosters and plays well below his ratings. The only way to get him back to full energy is a timeout. This mod started as a fix for that.

## How fatigue is stored

Each team has a fatigue table with one byte per roster slot. 0 means fresh and 255 means fully tired. Team Rosters shows this as ENERGY: 100% at 0, and about 0.3% less for each point.

## How fatigue lowers ratings

At every snap, the game copies each player’s ratings onto the field and lowers them by his fatigue. Above about 94% energy, the ratings don’t change. Below that, every rating except weight goes down: at 88% energy a player plays at about 95% of his ratings, at 75% at about 82%, and at 0% at about a third.

## What added fatigue in the original game

Fatigue came only from a few events. Each one added a fixed amount:

| Event | Fatigue added |
|---|---|
| Ball carrier tackled | 40 |
| Making the tackle | 30 |
| Engaging a block (blocker and defender) | 20 |
| A run tick for the ball carrier | 10 |
| A receiver’s route ending | 10 |
| Spins and other moves | 2 to 5 |

A short carry cost a halfback 90 to 110 points, about 27% of his energy. In CPU-vs-CPU games we measured, a halfback averaged 42 points per play, a fullback 17, a lineman 9 and a cornerback 5.

## How players recovered in the original game

Recovery used a timer that ticks about once a second on the play-call screen, at the line and during the play. At each snap, every player got back half a point per tick. A typical play takes about 24 ticks, so every player got back about 12 points per play.

A halfback lost about 100 points per carry and got back 12. Linemen and defenders lost less than 12 points per play, so they stayed at 100% all game.

The problem: every player recovered the same flat amount, but one position lost far more than the others. No single recovery amount works for both. A larger amount fixes the halfback, but then nobody else ever gets tired. A smaller amount makes the halfback even worse.

## What Elevated changed

Elevated keeps the fatigue table and the way fatigue lowers ratings. It changes how fatigue is added and recovered:

- **Fatigue comes from what a player does.** During the play, every player gets tired from the distance he runs (based on his speed, every frame), the time he spends in a block, and the original events at a quarter of their old amounts.
- **At every snap, each player recovers a quarter of his fatigue.** It is not a flat amount: a fresh player gets back a little, a tired player gets back a lot.
- **Timeouts and halftime** still reset every player to full energy.

As a result, a halfback who carries three times in a row is tired, a receiver who runs deep routes on every play of a drive is tired, and linemen get tired in long drives. On a 6-button pad, hold Z before the snap to see every player’s energy.
